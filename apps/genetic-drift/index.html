How this organism works

Load a few long recordings — field recordings, room tone, tape reels, anything. A small population of organisms takes over: each one holds a 16-cell genome and an elementary cellular-automaton rule. When an organism is ready to play, it picks a sample and a random start point, cuts a random-length excerpt from it — anywhere from under a second to several minutes — and fades it into the mix. The moment that excerpt ends, the organism evolves its genome, decides whether to play again or fall dormant for a while, and the cycle continues, unsynchronized from every other voice. The instrument drifts through four structural phases that lean the whole population toward short flickers or long suspended drones; most transitions arrive gradually, some snap without warning. Pitch here moves the way tape pitch always has — speed and pitch travel together — and Timing chaos can make an onset hesitate, rush, or shatter into a quick stutter instead of one clean start. Doubles is the odd one out: switch it on and some excerpts drag a second, independently-timed one along with them. The Mixer at the end gives every organism its own live fader, pan, and depth nudge, plus one master speed control that runs the whole colony faster or slower, tape-style. Nothing here loops — every excerpt is a fresh, independent draw.
